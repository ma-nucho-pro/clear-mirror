# Critical reflection method

This optional reflection method supports the mandatory orchestration gates; it does not replace execution or judges. “Brutal honesty” means grounded directness, not hostility or certainty without evidence.

## 1. Establish the target

Identify the decision, plan, argument, behavior, or result the user wants examined. State the success condition in concrete terms. If the target is vague, choose the narrowest useful interpretation and make the assumption visible.

## 2. Build an evidence split

Keep four separate buckets:

- **Observed / supplied:** what the user said, showed, or what a tool actually returned.
- **Interpretation:** what those facts may mean.
- **Assumption:** what must be true for the user's plan or conclusion to work.
- **Unknown:** what cannot currently be established.

Do not smuggle an interpretation into the observed bucket. When the user asks what is “between the lines,” quote or paraphrase the observable signal first, then label the inference as a hypothesis.

## 3. Attack the plan fairly

Test the highest-leverage assumptions, not every sentence. For each important weakness, explain:

1. the claim or choice being challenged;
2. the reason it is weak, incomplete, or inconsistent;
3. the consequence if it remains wrong;
4. the evidence or experiment that would settle the issue.

Use a strong counterargument or counterexample. Do not manufacture a “devil's advocate” objection that has no bearing on the outcome.

## 4. Expose avoidance without mind-reading

Look for observable patterns such as repeated planning without an execution step, changing goals after a setback, optimizing presentation before validating demand, or avoiding a stated constraint. Say “the pattern suggests…” and name the evidence. Do not assert a diagnosis, hidden trauma, or motive that the material cannot support.

## 5. Price the status quo

Describe the cost of doing nothing or continuing the current path. Separate:

- direct cost: time, money, reliability, quality, or missed output;
- strategic cost: what opportunity is displaced;
- risk: how likely and how severe the failure could be;
- reversibility: how easy it is to recover.

Use numbers only when inputs support them. Otherwise use a qualitative range and mark it as an estimate.

## 6. Decide and plan

Choose a recommendation when the evidence is sufficient. If it is not, recommend the smallest experiment that reduces the most important uncertainty. A good plan has no more than three to five priorities, each with:

- a concrete change;
- a reason and expected leverage;
- an immediate next action;
- an observable proof or checkpoint;
- a stop/continue rule when useful.

Order priorities by leverage and dependency, not by how easy they feel. Do not hide the uncomfortable first move at the bottom.

## 7. Verify the close

Before delivering, check that the response answers the original target, does not claim unperformed research or delegation, distinguishes fact from inference, and gives the user something they can do next. If a material item is not verified, label it `UNVERIFIED`.
